5 · Printable Glossary Card

Every tai chi term used in the book, in one place — sourced word for word from the back of the book. Print it and keep it nearby the first few times a term comes up mid-practice.

Brush Knee and Twist Step — a standing posture in the Simplified 24-Form in which the practitioner steps forward while one hand sweeps down and past the knee and the other pushes forward at shoulder height.
Cheng Man-ch'ing's 37 Postures — a 37-posture shortened form of Yang style, created by Cheng Man-ch'ing in mainland China by the mid-1940s, brought to Taiwan in 1949 and later to the United States in the 1960s; kept distinct in this book from the Simplified 24-Form.
Commencing Form — the opening movement of the Simplified 24-Form, also called the Preparation Form, that introduces the coordination of rising and lowering arms with bending knees.
Dan tien — a traditional focal point located roughly one to two inches below the navel, used to direct attention and initiate movement; presented in the book as traditional teaching, not as a measurable anatomical structure.
Empty leg — the leg carrying little to none of the practitioner's weight during a posture, left free to step, shift, or extend.
Golden Rooster Stands on One Leg — a standing posture in which one leg supports the full body weight while the other knee lifts to roughly hip height, described in traditional teaching as rooted.
Grasp the Bird's Tail — a posture appearing in various forms throughout the Simplified 24-Form, moving through four connected actions: ward off, roll back, press, and push.
Grounding — a traditional term, used interchangeably with “rooted,” for the sense of a stable stance connected through the standing leg into the floor.
Part the Wild Horse's Mane — an early posture in the Simplified 24-Form in which the practitioner steps out to the side while the hands separate, as though parting an imaginary ball held at the hip.
Rooted / Rootedness — traditional language for a stable, connected stance in which weight settles fully through the standing leg into the ground, rather than sitting rigidly on top of the joints.
Seated tai chi — tai chi practiced from a chair instead of standing; presented throughout the book as an equally legitimate, complete form of practice rather than a modified or lesser version.
Simplified 24-Form — a standardized 24-movement sequence, drawn primarily from Yang style, created in 1956 by a committee under China's national sports authority for mass public health use; also called the Beijing Form, the 24-Posture Form, or Simplified Taijiquan.
Single Whip — a posture in which the practitioner extends into a wide stance with weight almost entirely on one leg while the opposite arm extends fully to the side into a relaxed “hook hand.”
Taijiquan — the formal Chinese name for tai chi; the book notes the Simplified 24-Form is also called “Simplified Taijiquan.”
Wave Hands Like Clouds — a posture in which the hands trace slow, circular paths in front of the body while the weight shifts rhythmically from side to side; often described by practitioners as one of the calmest movements in the form.
Weight-bearing leg — the leg currently holding most of the practitioner's weight during a posture or weight shift.
Yang style — the tai chi tradition developed by Yang Luchan in the early-to-mid 1800s, adapted from the Chen family's martial art into smoother, more evenly paced movement; the source tradition for the Simplified 24-Form taught in this book.
